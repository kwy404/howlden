A group video call with nothing to sign up for

Sometimes you just want to see a few faces: grandparents on a birthday, old friends on a Sunday evening, a project group the night before a deadline. The usual obstacle is not the call itself, it is the setup. Someone has no account, someone forgot their password, someone does not want to hand a phone number to yet another service. howlden skips that part entirely. It runs in the browser on desktop and phone, there is no account, no email and no phone number, and nothing to install. You pick a display name and an avatar, they stay in your own browser, and you are ready.

This guide is about the video side: how to start a call, what to expect from your connection, and when a different kind of tool is the better pick. Think of it as gathering the pack around a campfire, only with cameras.

Starting a video call in five steps

  1. Open the app on a computer or phone and choose a name and an avatar.
  2. Create a den. A den is a small group space with text channels, voice channels and a member list, like a tiny private server.
  3. Invite people. Create an invite and howlden turns it into a link and a QR code. Send the link through WhatsApp, Signal, email or any messenger you already use, or let the person scan the QR code if you are in the same room.
  4. Get the reply code back. When your friend opens the link, their browser makes a short reply code. They send it back the same way, you paste or scan it, and they are in. Each newcomer needs this one exchange with a member, and howlden then connects them to everyone else in the den automatically.
  5. Join the voice channel and turn on your camera. Video and screen share live inside voice channels. Your browser asks for camera permission the first time.

The single round trip of invite and reply is the price of having no server. It takes a minute the first time and feels natural after that. If a link does not open properly inside some messenger, there is always a manual paste option.

Where your video goes, and where it does not

In a howlden call, video does not pass through a company server. Every member has a direct, end to end encrypted connection to every other member, a setup called a full mesh. WebRTC, the technology built into every modern browser, encrypts each connection with DTLS and the audio and video with SRTP, and that cannot be switched off. There is no howlden backend, database or analytics, so we keep no recording or log of your call anywhere.

If you want extra assurance, howlden shows a short connection fingerprint. Compare it out loud at the start of the call: if it matches on both screens, nobody is sitting in the middle. You can read more about this in the guide to private group voice calls.

The honest part: upload bandwidth

A mesh has one real cost. Your browser sends its own copy of your video to each other person in the den, so the more cameras are on, the more you upload. With a few people, a typical home connection usually copes well. As you approach the den limit of 8 people, everyone sends up to seven video streams at once, and a slow upload line or an older phone will start to struggle: video gets blurry, freezes, or the device gets warm.

Audio is far lighter than video, so a few habits keep things smooth:

  • Let people on weak connections join with voice only. They still receive everyone else's video, which costs download rather than upload.
  • Turn cameras on for the moments that matter, like the cake or the big reveal, and off again afterwards.
  • Plug in the laptop, and close other video apps and large downloads.
  • If someone keeps freezing, try a wired connection or move closer to the router.
  • For photos or slides, share your screen instead of holding things up to the camera. See screen share without an app for how that works.

Mesh or server: picking the right approach

Tools like Zoom, Google Meet and Jitsi use a server based approach: each person sends one video stream to a media server, which forwards it to everyone else. That is excellent for big meetings, webinars and classes, and it lets people join through a single meeting link with no codes to swap. howlden takes the direct approach, which suits small private groups. Neither is better in general. They are built for different jobs.

If you regularly need twenty people on camera, a server based service is the right tool. If you want four friends on a call with no accounts and no middleman, the mesh is a very good fit.

A few more things worth knowing

  • Everyone needs to be online at once. That is true of any video call, but here it also applies to the text channels: there is no server keeping messages for people who arrive later.
  • Some networks block direct calls. If two people are both on strict networks, such as some offices, schools, hotels or mobile carriers, the call may fail to connect. howlden explains what happened in plain words and lets you add your own TURN relay in settings. The relay only forwards encrypted packets and cannot see your video.
  • Your device still matters. End to end encryption protects the connection, not a compromised computer or phone. Keep your browser updated.
  • People in the call can still record. As with any call, someone could capture their own screen, so share with people you trust.
