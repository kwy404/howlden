Two ways to send a message

Almost every chat app you have used works like a post office. You write a message, your phone uploads it to the company's servers, and those servers deliver it to your friend, often keeping a copy so it can show up later on another device. That design is good at a lot of things: huge groups, message history, people who are offline for days.

Peer to peer (often shortened to P2P) works more like two people passing notes across a table. Your device sends the message straight to your friend's device. There is no post office in the middle sorting the mail. In howlden, that is true for text, voice, video, screen share and files: they all go from browser to browser.

The idea sounds simple, but the internet was not built to make it easy. The interesting part of P2P chat is everything that has to happen before those two devices can talk.

Problem one: finding each other

Your laptop does not have a public phone number on the internet. Behind your router it has a private address that only makes sense inside your home network. Your router uses something called NAT (network address translation) to share one public address among every device in the house. That is great for the router, and awkward for anyone trying to reach your laptop from outside.

So the first question a browser asks is: "what does my address look like from the outside?" To answer it, howlden asks a public STUN server (Google runs free ones). The STUN server replies with your public address and port, and that is all it does. It never sees your messages or your calls, and it is not involved once the conversation starts.

Your browser then gathers a few possible routes to reach you (local address, public address, and a relay route if you configured one) and packs them into a connection offer. In WebRTC terms these routes are called ICE candidates, if you want to read more on MDN.

Problem two: the handshake

Both browsers need each other's offer before they can connect. Most P2P apps solve this with a "signaling server" that quietly passes the offers back and forth. howlden has no server at all, so it lets you be the messenger. That is why inviting someone takes one round trip:

  1. A den member creates an invite. howlden compresses the connection offer into a link and a QR code. You send the link through any messenger you already use, or let your friend scan the QR.
  2. Your friend opens the link. Their browser builds a short reply code, also a link or QR, and sends it back the same way.
  3. You paste or scan the reply code. The two browsers now know how to reach each other, and they connect directly. If scanning is awkward, a manual paste option is always there.

You never see raw technical text, only a link or a picture. It is a small extra step compared to a server based app, and it is also the reason nobody can wander into a den uninvited: without that exchange, there is no way in.

Problem three: keeping the line private

Once a route is found, WebRTC sets up encryption before anything else flows. Data channels (text and files) use DTLS, and audio and video use SRTP with keys agreed through that DTLS handshake. This is built into every modern browser and cannot be switched off. The result is that the connection is end to end encrypted between the two browsers.

How do you know the other end is really your friend and not someone sitting in between? howlden shows a short connection fingerprint. Read it aloud on the call or compare it on screen. If both of you see the same thing, the line is the one you think it is.

Want a second lock? A den can have an optional passphrase. howlden derives a key from it with PBKDF2 and adds an AES-GCM layer to messages and files, so only people who know the passphrase can read them. The encrypted group chat guide covers this in detail.

From two people to a whole pack

A chat between two people is one connection. A den with five people is a mesh: every member keeps a direct, encrypted connection to every other member. When a new friend joins through an invite, howlden uses the connections that already exist to introduce them to everyone else automatically, so you only do the link and reply code dance once per newcomer.

A mesh has a cost that grows with the group. If you share your camera in a den of five, your device sends your video four separate times, once to each friend. Audio is light, so a voice chat stays comfortable, but video eats upload bandwidth fast. That is why a howlden den holds up to 5 people. Bigger groups are a job for a server based tool, which can receive one stream and copy it out to hundreds of listeners.

When the direct path fails

Most home and mobile networks let two browsers connect directly after the STUN step. Some networks are stricter: certain office, school, hotel and mobile carrier networks use what is called strict NAT, and when both people are behind one, the direct route can fail.

The fix is a TURN relay, a server that forwards packets between the two sides. Because the traffic is already encrypted, the relay only sees scrambled packets and cannot read them. howlden notices when a connection fails, tells you in plain words, and lets you add your own TURN server in settings, stored only in your browser. The strict NAT guide walks through it.

The honest trade offs

Peer to peer is not "better" in every way. It is a different approach, and it shines in some places and not in others.

QuestionServer based chatPeer to peer (howlden)
Who holds your messages?The service's serversOnly the devices in the den
Friend offline?Message waits on the serverEveryone must be online at the same time
Group sizeCan be very largeUp to 5 people
Account needed?Usually yesNo, just a display name in your browser
JoiningSearch or a public invite linkOne round trip invite, nobody else can find the den

Two more things worth saying plainly. Encryption protects the connection, not your device: if a phone or laptop is compromised, no protocol can fix that. And because nothing is stored online, your name, settings and den list live in your browser's local storage, so clearing site data clears them too.
