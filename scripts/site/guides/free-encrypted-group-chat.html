What "encrypted" should mean for a group chat

Lots of apps say they are encrypted, and the word covers very different things. Sometimes it only means the link between your phone and the company's server is protected, while the server itself can read everything. That is called encryption in transit, and it is useful, but it still puts a readable copy of your chat somewhere you do not control.

End to end encryption is the stronger promise: data is encrypted on the sender's device and only decrypted on the receiver's device. Anything in the middle sees scrambled bytes. Apps like Signal and WhatsApp offer end to end encryption on top of a server that stores and delivers messages, which is how they reach people who are offline.

howlden takes a different route: there is no message server at all. Each member's browser talks directly to each other member's browser, and each of those direct connections is end to end encrypted. Below is exactly how, layer by layer.

Three layers of protection in a den

Layer one: WebRTC encryption, always on

Every connection in a den uses WebRTC, the real time standard built into modern browsers. WebRTC encrypts text and files with DTLS and audio and video with SRTP. This is part of the standard, so it cannot be turned off by us, by you, or by a misconfigured setting. If you want to read the details, the webrtc.org site is a good starting point.

Layer two: an optional den passphrase

You can give a den a passphrase. howlden runs it through PBKDF2 to derive a key, then encrypts messages and files a second time with AES-GCM before they even enter the WebRTC connection. Only members who know the passphrase can read them. Share the passphrase in person or over a channel different from the one you used for the invite link, so one leaked chat does not reveal both.

Layer three: a fingerprint you can check

Encryption is only as good as knowing who is on the other end. howlden shows a short connection fingerprint. Read it aloud on a voice call or compare it side by side on screen. If both sides match, nobody slipped into the middle of your connection when you exchanged invites.

Why it can be free (and stay free)

Running a chat service usually costs money because someone has to pay for servers that store messages, relay calls and keep accounts. Those costs are why many services need ads, subscriptions or data.

howlden avoids almost all of that by design:

  • The website is static files on GitHub Pages. There is no howlden backend, no database, no analytics and no tracking.
  • Messages, voice, video and files go directly between browsers, so there is nothing for us to store or relay.
  • There are no accounts. Your display name, avatar, settings and den list live in your browser's local storage.
  • The code is open source under the MIT license, so anyone can read how it works.

The only outside helper is a public STUN server (Google's free ones), which answers one question: what is my public address? It never sees your messages or calls.

What encryption cannot do

An honest security page lists the gaps too. Here are the ones that matter for a small private group:

  • Your device is the weak point. If a phone or laptop in the den has malware, or someone else is holding it unlocked, they can read what that member reads. Keep devices updated and locked.
  • Members can share what they see. Any member can screenshot, copy or forward a message. Encryption keeps outsiders out; it does not control insiders.
  • Direct connections reveal network addresses. In a peer to peer connection, the devices learn each other's internet address so they can talk. That is another reason to invite only people you actually know.
  • Relays see that traffic exists. If you add a TURN relay for a strict network, it forwards encrypted packets it cannot read, but it can see that you are connected and how much data flows.
  • Nothing is a magic shield. No chat tool is perfectly secure. howlden aims to remove the middle server and use strong, standard encryption, and to be clear about the rest.

A checklist for a den you can trust

  1. Send each invite one to one. Invites in howlden are already invite only, and nobody can find a den without one. Do not post invite links in public groups.
  2. Set a passphrase for dens where the topic is sensitive, and share it through a different path than the link.
  3. Compare fingerprints the first time someone joins, especially if the invite traveled through an app you do not fully trust.
  4. Keep the group small and known. A den holds up to 8 people anyway, which suits close friends, a family, a project team or a study group.
  5. Remember that nobody stores history for you. Everyone has to be online at the same time to receive messages, and clearing your browser data clears your den list.
